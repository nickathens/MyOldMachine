# Screenwriting Craft Reference

Principles of writing that makes people feel something.

## Dialogue

### The Golden Rule
**Nobody says anything unless they want something.** (David Mamet)

Every line of dialogue is an action: a character is trying to get, avoid, convince, deflect, seduce, intimidate, or hide something. If a line doesn't serve a want, cut it.

### Subtext
What characters mean is rarely what they say. Subtext is the engine of great dialogue.

**Bad (on the nose):**
```fountain
MARIA
I'm angry at you for leaving me.
```

**Good (subtext):**
```fountain
MARIA
You left the stove on again.
```

The argument isn't about the stove. It never was. The audience knows. That's the power.

### Techniques
- **Each character has a distinct voice.** Read the dialogue aloud with the character names covered. You should be able to tell who's talking from word choice, rhythm, and sentence length alone
- **People interrupt.** They trail off. They change topics mid-sentence. They avoid the question. Realistic dialogue is messy
- **Fewer words, more power.** "Get out" hits harder than "I would really appreciate it if you would please leave my house"
- **Let silence do the work.** A character not answering a question is an answer
- **Avoid expository dialogue.** Never have characters tell each other things they both already know. "As you know, Bob, our company was founded in 1985..."
- **Conflict in every exchange.** If two characters agree, the scene is dead. Find the friction. Even allies disagree on method

### Parentheticals
Use sparingly. Only when the tone is genuinely ambiguous.

**Necessary:**
```fountain
OFFICER
(smiling)
You're going away for a long time.
```

**Unnecessary:**
```fountain
OFFICER
(angrily)
You killed my partner!
```
The anger is obvious from the words and context.

## Action Lines

### Show, Don't Tell
The screenplay is a visual medium. Everything the audience knows, they learn by watching.

**Bad:** "Sarah feels anxious about the interview."
**Good:** "Sarah checks her watch for the third time. Her knee bounces under the desk."

**Bad:** "John is a veteran who has seen too much."
**Good:** "John's hands are steady as he field-strips the rifle. His eyes are not."

### Best Practices
- **Present tense, always.** "He walks" not "He walked"
- **Short paragraphs.** 3-4 lines maximum. White space is your friend. Dense blocks of action make readers skim
- **Strong, specific verbs.** "She storms into the room" not "She enters the room quickly"
- **Fragment sentences are fine.** "Silence. Then: gunfire." Reads faster than full sentences
- **No camera directions.** "We see" and "CLOSE ON" and "The camera pans" are not your job. Describe what happens, let the director decide how to shoot it
- **No internal states.** You cannot film thoughts. "She remembers her father" is unfilmable. "She stares at an old photograph" is filmable
- **Sound matters.** "The clock ticks. A dog barks three blocks away." Sound is visual storytelling through the ears

### Entering a Scene
"Enter late, exit early." Start the scene at the last possible moment. If two characters are meeting for coffee to discuss a plan, don't show them arriving, ordering, and making small talk. Start when the plan is already on the table.

## Pacing

### Page = Minute Rule
One properly formatted screenplay page equals roughly one minute of screen time. Use this to gauge pacing:
- If your setup is 15 pages for a short film, that's 15 minutes before anything happens
- If a dialogue scene runs 5 pages, that's 5 minutes of two people talking
- Action sequences read fast but play slow; dialogue reads slow but plays fast

### Rhythm
- **Vary scene length.** Short-long-short-long creates rhythm. Three long scenes in a row is a flatline
- **Tension and release.** Build pressure, then give the audience a moment to breathe. Then build again harder
- **Ticking clocks.** Deadlines create urgency. "The bomb goes off in ten minutes" accelerates everything
- **Parallel editing.** Cutting between two storylines can multiply tension

### The "What If I Cut This?" Test
For every scene, ask: if I remove this scene entirely, does the story still make sense? If yes, cut it. Every scene must either:
1. Advance the plot (cause the next scene)
2. Reveal character (show us who someone really is under pressure)
3. Establish essential information (but only if no other scene does this)

## Character

### The Character Diamond
Every compelling character needs:
1. **A want** (external goal: what they're pursuing)
2. **A need** (internal truth: what they must learn)
3. **A flaw** (the gap between want and need: what holds them back)
4. **A ghost** (backstory wound: why the flaw exists)

The want drives the plot. The need drives the theme. The flaw creates obstacles. The ghost creates empathy.

### Revealing Character
Character is not what someone says about themselves. It's what they do when no one's watching. It's the choice they make when both options cost something.

**Weak:** A character says "I'm brave."
**Strong:** A character who's clearly terrified opens the door anyway.

### Antagonists
The antagonist believes they're the hero of their own story. The best antagonists have a legitimate point. They're wrong about something essential, but their logic is internally consistent.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Opening with an alarm clock.** The character wakes up, gets ready, drives to work. This is not a movie. Start in the middle of something interesting
2. **Flashbacks as crutch.** If the backstory is more interesting than the present, you're telling the wrong story
3. **Villain monologues.** No one explains their plan to the person trying to stop them
4. **Resolving tension too quickly.** The audience wants to sit in discomfort. Let the awkward silence last. Let the misunderstanding fester
5. **"And then" plotting.** Each scene should cause the next: "therefore" or "but" connections, never "and then." (Trey Parker/Matt Stone rule)
6. **Over-describing appearances.** "She's 5'7", 135 lbs, with auburn hair and green eyes." The casting director decides this. Give us the vibe: "She looks like she hasn't slept in a week and doesn't care."
7. **Telling the audience what to feel.** Music cues, on-the-nose dialogue, voiceover that explains the theme. Trust the audience. They're smarter than you think
8. **Too many characters.** If three characters serve the same function, combine them into one
9. **No stakes.** If the hero fails, what happens? If the answer is "nothing much," there's no movie
10. **Writing is rewriting.** The first draft is never the final draft. The real work begins in revision
