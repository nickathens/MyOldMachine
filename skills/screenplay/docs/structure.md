# Story Structure Reference

Multiple structural frameworks for organizing a screenplay. Choose based on the story's needs.

## 1. Classical Three-Act Structure (Syd Field Paradigm)

The foundation. Introduced by Syd Field in "Screenplay" (1979).

```
ACT 1 (Setup)          ACT 2 (Confrontation)              ACT 3 (Resolution)
~25%                    ~50%                                ~25%
pp. 1-30               pp. 30-90                           pp. 90-120

   Inciting        Plot Point 1   Midpoint   Plot Point 2     Climax
   Incident            |             |            |              |
   ~p.12              ~p.25-30      ~p.60       ~p.85-90       ~p.110
```

**Act 1 (Setup, ~30 pages):**
Introduce the world, the protagonist, their flaw, the stakes. End with Plot Point 1: a major event that spins the story in a new direction and forces the hero into Act 2.

**Act 2 (Confrontation, ~60 pages):**
The longest and hardest to write. The hero pursues their goal, encounters escalating obstacles. Divided by the Midpoint into 2A and 2B.
- **2A (Fun and Games):** The hero explores the new world/situation
- **Midpoint:** Raises stakes. False victory or false defeat
- **2B (Closing In):** Pressure mounts, allies may fail, plan unravels
- **Plot Point 2:** All seems lost. Hero must make a final choice

**Act 3 (Resolution, ~30 pages):**
The hero confronts the antagonist with a new understanding gained from the journey. Climax resolves the central dramatic question. Denouement shows the new normal.

**Pinch Points (Field's later addition):**
- Pinch 1: Halfway between PP1 and Midpoint. Reminds us of the antagonist's power
- Pinch 2: Halfway between Midpoint and PP2. Increases desperation

## 2. Save the Cat Beat Sheet (Blake Snyder)

15 beats mapped to a 110-page screenplay. Works well for mainstream/commercial storytelling.

### ACT 1

| Beat | Position | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| **Opening Image** | 1% (~p.1) | Snapshot of the hero's world before transformation. Sets tone |
| **Theme Stated** | 5% (~p.5) | Someone (not the hero) states the story's thematic question |
| **Setup** | 1-10% (pp.1-10) | The hero's status quo: flaws, relationships, "things that need fixing" |
| **Catalyst** | 12% (~p.12) | The inciting incident. Life will never be the same |
| **Debate** | 12-25% (pp.12-25) | Hero hesitates, resists, questions. Should I? Can I? |

### ACT 2A

| Beat | Position | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| **Break Into Two** | 25% (~p.25) | Hero commits to the journey. Crosses the threshold |
| **B Story** | 30% (~p.30) | Subplot begins (often love interest). Carries the theme |
| **Fun and Games** | 30-55% (pp.30-55) | The "promise of the premise." What the trailer shows |
| **Midpoint** | 55% (~p.55) | False victory or false defeat. Stakes raised. "Fun" ends |

### ACT 2B

| Beat | Position | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| **Bad Guys Close In** | 55-75% (pp.55-75) | External pressures mount. Internal team/relationships fracture |
| **All Is Lost** | 75% (~p.75) | Rock bottom. The "whiff of death" (literal or metaphorical) |
| **Dark Night of the Soul** | 75-85% (pp.75-85) | Mourning. Despair. Wallowing. What have I become? |

### ACT 3

| Beat | Position | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| **Break Into Three** | 85% (~p.85) | A revelation. The hero sees what they missed all along |
| **Finale** | 85-99% (pp.85-110) | Applying the lesson. Defeating the bad guys. Transformation earned |
| **Final Image** | 100% (~p.110) | Mirror of the opening. Shows how far the hero has come |

## 3. Dan Harmon's Story Circle

8 steps in a circle. Simplified from Joseph Campbell's Hero's Journey. Great for episodic content, short films, and character-driven stories.

```
        1. YOU (comfort zone)
      /                        \
   8. CHANGED                2. NEED
   (return, transformed)     (want something)
      |                        |
   7. RETURN                 3. GO
   (back to familiar)       (enter unfamiliar)
      |                        |
   6. TAKE                   4. SEARCH
   (pay the price)          (adapt, struggle)
      \                        /
        5. FIND (get what they wanted)
```

| Step | Question | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 1. YOU | Who is the character? | Establish the character in their zone of comfort/familiarity |
| 2. NEED | What do they want? | A desire or need disrupts the status quo |
| 3. GO | They enter an unfamiliar situation | Cross the threshold. Leave what's known |
| 4. SEARCH | Adapt to it | Struggle, learn the rules of the new world |
| 5. FIND | Get what they wanted | Achieve the goal (or think they did) |
| 6. TAKE | Pay a heavy price | It cost something. Nothing comes free |
| 7. RETURN | Back to the familiar situation | Return to the starting world |
| 8. CHANGED | Having changed | The character is fundamentally different |

**Top half** (1-4) = descent into chaos. **Bottom half** (5-8) = ascent back.
**Left side** (8,1,2) = order/thesis. **Right side** (4,5,6) = chaos/antithesis.
The transitions (3 and 7) are the crossing points.

## 4. Short Film Structure

Short films (5-15 pages) demand compression. Every line must earn its place.

**The compressed arc:**
```
Setup (10-15%)  ->  Inciting Incident  ->  Rising Action  ->  Climax  ->  Resolution (5-10%)
```

**Key principles:**
- Enter late, exit early. Don't establish what the audience can infer
- One central conflict. One dramatic question. One transformation
- 2-4 characters maximum. More than that and you're spending runtime on introductions
- 1-3 locations. Each new location costs screen time for establishing shots
- The inciting incident should happen on page 1 or 2, not page 5
- The ending can be ambiguous. Short films reward audiences who think
- Subtext over exposition. Every word of dialogue must do at least two jobs

**Common short film structures:**
1. **Single situation**: One scene, one location, one conflict resolved (or not)
2. **Twist ending**: Setup creates an assumption, the ending subverts it
3. **Character study**: Less about plot, more about revealing who someone is under pressure
4. **Circular**: End where you began, but the meaning has changed
5. **Escalation**: One simple thing goes wrong, then worse, then catastrophic

## Choosing a Structure

| Story Type | Best Framework |
|-----------|---------------|
| Mainstream feature film | Save the Cat or Syd Field |
| Character study | Story Circle |
| TV episode | Story Circle (per act) or mini-Save the Cat |
| Short film (under 10 min) | Compressed 3-act or circular |
| Comedy sketch | Setup/escalation/punchline (no formal structure needed) |
| Experimental/arthouse | Break all of the above deliberately |
